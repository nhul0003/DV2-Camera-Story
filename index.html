A data story about cameras · Australia · 1840–2025

From Film to Phone

How the camera changed over 150 years, and what the switch from film to digital cost Australia.

This story follows cameras through museum collections, trade records and a database of digital cameras. It shows what people used in the film era, how the film industry collapsed, how digital cameras boomed and faded, and how phones replaced them. Along the way, it asks what each change meant for Australia.

01

The film era

For a century, cameras kept changing shape

Glass plates gave way to box, folding, roll-film and 35mm cameras. Each new type took over from the one before. The darker a square, the bigger that type's share of the decade's cameras.

1 · Roll film was the top type in every decade from the 1900s to the 1930s, peaking at 46% in the 1910s. 2 · 35mm led the 1960s with 35%.

3 · Digital was already 65% of the 1990s, the darkest square on the chart.

Where Melbourne's cameras came from

Most cameras in Museums Victoria were made overseas. Every line ends at the museum in Melbourne, and the thicker the line, the more cameras came from that country. The USA is the biggest source, with 65 of 163 cameras (40%), 38 of them by Kodak. Japanese cameras only appear after 1950: drag across 1950–1990 on the bar chart to see them. 18 were made in Australia, 11 by Kodak Australasia in Melbourne.

02

The film industry collapses

Film cameras almost vanished in ten years

In 2000 the world exported US$3.6 billion of 35mm film cameras, 55% of it from Hong Kong and China. By 2010 the trade had fallen 96%, to US$158 million, as digital cameras took over. Australia, in red, followed the same path: its exports fell 98%, from US$5.2m to US$0.1m. Choose a year under the map to watch the circles shrink inside their 2000 outlines.

Australia was a top-10 film maker

In 2000, Australia ranked 9th in the world for colour film exports, ahead of Singapore.

By 2005 it had dropped to 17th, and by 2015 to 31st, out of the top 20 altogether.

Then the factory closed

In November 2004, Kodak shut its Coburg plant in Melbourne, which made film, photo paper and chemicals. Kodak blamed the switch to digital photography.

Australia's colour film exports fell 93% in five years, from US$74 million in 2000 to US$4.9 million in 2005.

03

The digital boom

A flood of new cameras, then a drought

Digital cameras needed no film, and makers raced to release new models. 2,575 of the 3,860 models came out in 2004–2012.

The peak was 2010, with 340 new models, almost one a day. Fujifilm alone released 62 that year (outlined).

By 2015 there were only 70, down 79%.

The megapixel race

Every year, new cameras packed more pixels onto their sensors. The typical new model had 2 megapixels in 2000, 12 in 2010 and 24 by 2019.

Then the race stalled. Since 2017, most new cameras have had about 20 to 26 megapixels.

Big names left the market

Of the 22 brands with at least 40 models, 13 have stopped, and every one released its last model by 2015.

Kodak, the oldest brand here, made 207 models from 1994 to 2014. Its busiest year was 2009.

The 9 still going are mostly camera and lens makers, and 8 of them are Japanese.

Small cameras disappeared first

During the boom, cameras got lighter. The typical 2005–2012 model weighed 150 g, small enough for a pocket.

After 2012 that peak of small cameras flattened out. The typical new model now weighs 370 g, about two and a half times as much.

A new kind of image sensor

Early digital cameras used CCD sensors. CMOS sensors use less power and became cheaper to make, and from 2011 they took over quickly.

CMOS passed half of all new models in 2012. Since 2017, every new model in the database uses one.

04

The phone takes over

Phones arrived as cameras peaked

Australians signed up for mobiles fast: 45 subscriptions per 100 people in 2000, 101 by 2007, more than one each.

Camera makers kept growing for three more years, to the 2010 peak. Then new models fell away, and landlines dropped from 53 to 22 per 100.

Almost everyone carries a phone

The map shades each country by the share of people who own a mobile phone, so countries of every size can be compared fairly. Worldwide it is 82%. Australia is at 97.5%, 28th of 185 countries, and has stayed between 94% and 98% since 2019. The lightest countries are in Central and East Africa: Burundi is lowest at 28%.

A camera in almost every Australian pocket

Out of every 100 Australians, 98 own a mobile phone. Worldwide it is 82, and in the least developed countries 63.

Almost every phone is also a camera, so most Australians now carry one everywhere they go.

What it meant for Australia

For a century, Australians bought cameras made overseas and film made in Melbourne. When digital arrived, Kodak's Coburg plant closed and the country's film exports fell 93% in five years. Digital cameras boomed and faded within a decade. Today the camera most Australians use is the phone in their pocket.
