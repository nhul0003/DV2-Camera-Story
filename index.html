A data story about cameras · Australia · 1840–2025

From Film to Phone

How the camera changed over 150 years, and what the switch from film to digital cost Australia.

This story follows cameras through museum collections, trade records and a database of digital cameras. It shows what people used in the film era, how the film industry collapsed, how digital cameras boomed and faded, and how phones replaced them. Along the way, it asks what each change meant for Australia.

01

The film era

For a century, cameras kept changing shape

Glass plates gave way to box cameras, then folding, roll-film and 35mm cameras. Each new type took over from the one before, and each made photography cheaper and easier. The darker a square, the bigger that type's share of all the cameras made in that decade.

1 · Roll film · 1910s
46% of the decade's cameras

Roll film was the top type in every decade from the 1900s to the 1930s. It could be loaded in daylight, so photography became a family hobby.

2 · 35mm · 1960s
35% of the decade's cameras

The most common camera of the 1960s. 35mm film became the standard for family cameras.

3 · Digital · 1990s
65% of the decade's cameras

The darkest square on the chart. In its first decade, digital was already two-thirds of the collection.

Where Melbourne's cameras came from

Most cameras in Museums Victoria were made overseas, first in the USA, Britain and Germany, later in Japan. Eighteen were made here, mostly by Kodak in Melbourne. Every line ends at the museum in Melbourne, and the thicker the line, the more cameras came from that country.

USA · 40%
65 of 163 cameras

The biggest source. 38 of them were made by Kodak, whose head office was in Rochester, New York.

Japan · after 1950
16 cameras, all from 1950 on

Japanese cameras only appear after the Second World War. Select 1950–1990 on the bar chart to see them.

Made in Australia · 18
11 by Kodak Australasia

Kodak built cameras in Melbourne for Australian families until its Coburg plant closed in 2004.

02

The film industry collapses

Film cameras almost vanished in ten years

In 2000 the world exported US$3.6 billion of 35mm film cameras, over half from Hong Kong and China. By 2010 the trade had fallen by 96%. Choose a year under the map to watch the circles shrink inside their 2000 outlines. Australia, in red, followed the same path.

2000 · US$3.6 billion
55% from Hong Kong and China

The last big year for film cameras. Hong Kong alone exported over US$1 billion worth.

2010 · down 96%
US$158 million worldwide

Digital cameras took over within a decade. By 2015 exports were just US$58 million.

Australia · down 98%
US$5.2m (2000) → US$0.1m (2010)

Australia's own film camera exports collapsed at the same pace, falling 98% in ten years.

Proportional symbol map · Circle area shows each country's exports in US$ (current prices); dashed outlines show 2000 for comparison · Choose a year below the map and hover over a circle for details · Source: UN Comtrade via World Bank WITS, HS code 900653 (cameras for 35mm roll film).

Australia was a top-10 film maker

In 2000, Australia ranked 9th in the world for colour film exports, ahead of Singapore.

By 2005 it had dropped to 17th, and by 2015 to 31st, out of the top 20 altogether.

Bump chart · Every rank from #1 to #31 is evenly spaced · Hover over a line to highlight it · Rank by export value of colour film for 35mm cameras · Mexico stops reporting after 2010 · Source: UN Comtrade via World Bank WITS, HS code 370254.

Then the factory closed

In November 2004, Kodak shut its Coburg plant in Melbourne, which made film, photo paper and chemicals. Kodak blamed the switch to digital photography.

Australia's colour film exports fell 93% in five years, from US$74 million in 2000 to US$4.9 million in 2005.

03

The digital boom

A flood of new cameras, then a drought

Digital cameras needed no film, and every photo could be checked straight away. Camera makers raced to release new models every year, and two-thirds of all models came out between 2004 and 2012. After 2010, the flood slowed to a trickle.

2010 · the peak
340 new models in one year

Almost one new camera every day. 2,575 of the 3,860 models came out in 2004–2012.

Fujifilm · 62 models
The busiest square, in 2010

A new Fujifilm model about every six days. Samsung released 48 that same year.

2015 · down 79%
Only 70 new models

Kodak and Casio released their last models in 2014, Samsung in 2015. Seven of these 15 brands have none after 2015.

Heatmap with a linked bar chart · Hover over a brand's row to show its models in the bar chart above · The 15 brands with the most models (85% of all models) · Source: CameraDatabase (leavestylecode, GitHub), 3,860 digital camera models, 1994–2025.

The megapixel race

Every year, new cameras packed more pixels onto their sensors. The typical new model had 2 megapixels in 2000, 12 in 2010 and 24 by 2019.

Then the race stalled. Since 2017, most new cameras have had about 20 to 26 megapixels.

Big names left the market

Twenty-two brands released at least 40 digital camera models. Thirteen of them have stopped, and every one of those had released its last model by 2015, within eight years of the first iPhone. Most brands had their busiest year around 2010, just before the fall.

13 of 22 · gone by 2015
No brand quit after 2015

Household names such as HP, Kodak, Casio and Samsung released their last models between 2010 and 2015.

Kodak · 1994–2014
207 models in 21 years

The oldest brand in the database. Its busiest year was 2009, only five years before its last model.

9 still going
8 of them from Japan

The survivors are mostly camera and lens makers, such as Canon, Nikon and Fujifilm, plus Sony and Panasonic.
