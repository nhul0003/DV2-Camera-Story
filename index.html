A data story about cameras · Australia · 1840–2025

From Film to Phone

How the camera changed over 150 years, and what the switch from film to digital cost Australia.

This story follows cameras through museum collections, trade records and a database of digital cameras. It shows what people used in the film era, how the film industry collapsed, how digital cameras boomed and faded, and how phones replaced them. Along the way, it asks what each change meant for Australia.

01

The film era

For a century, cameras kept changing shape

Glass plates gave way to box, folding, roll-film and 35mm cameras. Each new type took over from the one before. The darker a square, the bigger that type's share of the decade's cameras in the museum (what it holds, not what was sold).

1 · Roll film was the top type in every decade from the 1900s to the 1930s, peaking at 46% in the 1910s. 2 · 35mm led the 1960s with 35%.

Cheaper cameras came next. Pocket cameras were 31% of the 1970s, and disposables were 60% of the 2000s.

3 · Digital was already 65% of the 1990s, one of the darkest squares on the chart.

Where Melbourne's cameras came from

Most cameras in Museums Victoria were made overseas. Every line ends at the museum in Melbourne, and the thicker the line, the more cameras came from that country. The USA is the biggest source, with 65 of 163 cameras (40%), 38 of them by Kodak. Japanese cameras only appear after 1950: drag across 1950–1990 on the bar chart to see them. 18 were made in Australia, 11 by Kodak Australasia in Melbourne.

02

The film industry collapses

Film cameras almost vanished in ten years

In 2000 the world exported US$3.6 billion of 35mm film cameras, 55% of it from Hong Kong and China. By 2010 the trade had fallen 96%, to US$158 million, as digital cameras took over. Australia, in red, followed the same path: its exports fell 98%, from US$5.2m to US$0.1m. Choose a year under the map to watch the circles shrink inside their 2000 outlines.

Australia was a top-10 film maker

In 2000, Australia ranked 9th in the world for colour film exports, worth US$74 million. The leaders were Japan, the UK and the USA, each exporting over US$300 million.

Australia fell faster than the countries around it. By 2005 it had dropped to 17th, and by 2015 to 31st, when it exported just US$2,370 of colour film.

Its climb back to 18th in 2019 is not a recovery. Only 42 countries still reported exports that year, and Australia's were worth US$95,000.

The big makers shuffled at the top. China briefly led in 2005, and the USA has been first since 2019. Mexico's line ends in 2010, when it stopped reporting.

Then the factory closed

In November 2004, Kodak shut its Coburg plant in Melbourne, which made film, photo paper and chemicals. Kodak blamed the switch to digital photography.

Australia's colour film exports fell 93% in five years, from US$74 million in 2000 to US$4.9 million in 2005.

The fall did not stop there. By 2010 exports were US$234,000, and by 2015 just US$2,370. Without its own factory, Australia no longer made film to sell overseas.

The closure cost about 600 jobs in Melbourne's north.

03

The digital boom

A flood of new cameras, then a drought

Digital cameras needed no film, and makers raced to release new models. 2,575 of the 3,860 models came out in 2004–2012. Fujifilm made the most overall: 402 models.

The peak was 2010, with 340 new models, almost one a day. Fujifilm alone released 62 that year (outlined).

Samsung released 48 that same year. Hover over a brand to see its own boom and fall in the bars above.

By 2015 there were only 70 new models, down 79%. Since 2017 there have never been more than 37 in a year.

The megapixel race

Every year, new cameras packed more pixels onto their sensors. In 1996 the typical model had half a megapixel. It had 2 megapixels in 2000, 12 in 2010 and 24 by 2019.

The boxes were narrow during the boom: in 2010, the middle half of models had between 12 and 14 megapixels, so most cameras were very alike.

Then the race stalled. Since 2017, most new cameras have had about 20 to 26 megapixels. A few high-end models now reach 61.

Big names left the market

Each bar runs from a brand's first to last new model, and the black dot marks its busiest year. Of the 22 brands with at least 40 models, 13 have stopped, and every one released its last model by 2015.

Most brands peaked just before the fall: 16 of the 22 had their busiest year between 2006 and 2013.

Kodak, the oldest brand here, made 207 models from 1994 to 2014. Its busiest year was 2009.

Electronics brands left early: HP and Sanyo released their last models in 2010, and Casio in 2014.

The 9 still going are mostly camera and lens makers, and 8 of them are Japanese.

Small cameras disappeared first

Each curve shows how one era's models spread across weights, and the dashed lines mark each era's median. During the boom, cameras got lighter. The typical 2005–2012 model weighed 150 g, small enough for a pocket.

After 2012 that peak of small cameras flattened out. The typical new model now weighs 370 g, about two and a half times as much.

The heaviest models grew too. One in ten boom-era cameras weighed more than 445 g; since 2013 it is more than 743 g. The cameras still being made are the bigger ones.

A new kind of image sensor

Early digital cameras used CCD sensors. CMOS sensors use less power and became cheaper to make, and from 2011 they took over quickly.

As late as 2010, 82% of new models still used CCD. CMOS passed half of all new models in 2012, and reached 89% by 2015.

Since 2017, every new model in the database uses a CMOS sensor.

04

The phone takes over

Phones arrived as cameras peaked

Australians signed up for mobiles fast: 45 subscriptions per 100 people in 2000, 101 by 2007, more than one each.

Camera makers kept growing for three more years, to the 2010 peak of 340 new models. Then new models fell away: 70 in 2015 and 19 in 2024.

The home phone faded too. Landlines dropped from 53 to 22 per 100 people, while mobile subscriptions kept rising to 113 in 2024.

Almost everyone carries a phone

The map shades each country by the share of people who own a mobile phone, so countries of every size can be compared fairly. Worldwide it is 82%. Australia is at 97.5%, 28th of 185 countries, and has stayed between 94% and 98% since 2019. The lightest countries are in Central and East Africa: Burundi is lowest at 28%.

A camera in almost every Australian pocket

Out of every 100 Australians, 98 own a mobile phone. Worldwide it is 82, and in the least developed countries 63. Use the menu to compare Australia with another country, such as Indonesia (69) or New Zealand (96).

Almost every phone is also a camera, so most Australians now carry one everywhere they go.

This helps explain the earlier charts: the small, light cameras of the boom have almost disappeared from new releases.

What it meant for Australia

For a century, Australians bought cameras made overseas and film made in Melbourne. When digital arrived, Kodak's Coburg plant closed and the country's film exports fell 93% in five years. Digital cameras boomed and faded within a decade. Today the camera most Australians use is the phone in their pocket.
